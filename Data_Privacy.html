● What is Data Privacy?

Data Privacy is the protection of sensitive, personally identifiable information (PII) about individuals from unauthorized access, disclosure, or misuse. It governs how data are collected, stored, processed, and shared.
Privacy — We have knowledge of a person's identity, but NOT of an associated personal fact (e.g., we know who Alice is but not her illness).
Anonymity — We have knowledge of a personal fact, but NOT of the associated person's identity (e.g., we know someone has cancer but not who).
Key Concepts
  • PII (Personally Identifiable Information) — Any data that can directly or indirectly identify an individual: name, SSN, DOB, address, phone, email, income
  • Sweeney's Finding — ZIP code, date of birth, and gender are sufficient to uniquely identify 83% of the US population
  • Anonymization — A two-step process: (1) Data Masking on EI, (2) De-identification on QI fields
  • Data = Programs + Data + Documentation — Organizations holding customer data are its custodians and bear full legal responsibility
  • D′ = T(DQI) — The transformation function T is applied only on QI; EI is masked, SD is left unchanged
⭐ Exam Tip
Anonymization ≠ Privacy. Anonymization protects identity (EI + QI). Privacy protects sensitive data (SD). Understand which stakeholder needs which type of protection.

● Need for Sharing Data

Organizations share data to gain valuable insights. A healthcare provider's database on patient drug reactions is useful to pharmaceutical companies. However, these data cannot be shared directly due to legal, financial, compliance, and moral issues. Data must be shared responsibly — without revealing PII.
Use Cases for Data Sharing
Data Mining & Analysis

Classification (predict defaults), Clustering (segment customers), Association (market basket analysis).

Anonymization must preserve correlation between QI and SD for useful mining results.
Software Application Testing

High-quality test data = from production systems. Must be anonymized before sharing with testers.

Outsourced testing is common — compliance mandatory across geographies.
Business Operations (BPO)

BPOs assist customers in real-time. Sensitive data (credit card numbers, account details) must be tokenized dynamically so the BPO employee cannot see original values.
Classification of Use Cases
Privacy protection of data at rest (static)
Data Mining & Analysis, Software Testing — transform data offline before release
Privacy protection of data in motion (runtime)
Business Operations, Auditing & Reporting — tokenize data at the point of access, dynamically
⭐ Exam Tip
PII is found in ALL layers of enterprise architecture: business processes, applications, ODS, data warehouse, time-series databases, file servers, reports. An enterprise-wide privacy strategy is essential.

Anonymization: Constrained optimization — produce a dataset with smallest distortion that satisfies privacy requirements. "Shades of gray" — controllable privacy/utility levels.

● Importance of Balancing Privacy and Utility

Privacy vs Utility Trade-off: Anonymizing data always reduces some utility. The challenge is to maximize privacy while preserving enough utility for the data to be useful. This is a constrained optimization problem with no universal solution.
Key Relationships
  • D = [DEI] [DQI] [DSD] — Original data is a matrix of Explicit Identifiers, Quasi-Identifiers, and Sensitive Data
  • D′ = T(DQI) — Transformation applies only to QI; EI is masked, SD is unchanged
  • QI ↔ SD Correlation — Strong correlation exists between QI and SD. If correlation is lost during anonymization, data becomes useless for analysis
  • Measuring Privacy — Statistical approaches (variance of perturbed variables) and probabilistic methods (distribution knowledge + background info)
  • Measuring Utility — Statistical distance between Q (QI distribution in D) and Q′ (QI distribution in D′)
⭐ HIPAA Example — The Classic Trade-off
HIPAA designates admission date as PII → must be anonymized. But without admission date, healthcare researchers cannot assess treatment efficacy over time. HIPAA protects privacy but impacts medical research. This demonstrates that there is no single privacy vs utility measure — it depends on application context.

● Tabular Data

Tabular data are the most common output form from statistical databases. Two main types exist, each requiring different SDC methods.
Magnitude Tables

Each cell value = sum of a particular response across all respondents in that cell.

Commonly used for business/economic data.

Example: Total turnover of all businesses in an industry within a region.
Frequency Tables

Each cell value = count (number of respondents) falling into that cell.

Commonly used for Census/social data.

Example: Number of unemployed individuals within a region.
⭐ Exam Tip
Small values in tabular output signal potential disclosure risk. Cells with very few respondents may directly identify individuals. SDC must suppress or merge such cells.

● Approaches to Statistical Disclosure Control (SDC)

SDC is the set of methods to reduce the risk of disclosing information on individuals, businesses, or other organizations while releasing as much information as possible.
  1. Why is confidentiality protection needed? — Three reasons: (1) Fundamental principle for Official Statistics, (2) Public pledges to respondents, (3) Maintain trust and data quality
  2. Key characteristics and uses of data — Type (census/survey), sample design, quality assessment, variables (categorical/continuous), output type (microdata/tables)
  3. What disclosure risks need to be protected against? — Identify intruder scenarios: what info is available to attacker and how they would use it to identify an individual
  4. Disclosure control methods — Apply SDC methods to reduce risk to acceptable level; balance information loss against disclosure likelihood
  5. Implementation — Select software, parameters, options; document decisions; inform users about protection applied; be transparent but not enough to allow recovery of disclosive data
Perturbative Methods
Falsify data before publication by introducing controlled error.
Examples: Additive noise, data swapping, PRAM, rounding, microaggregation
Non-Perturbative Methods
Reduce information by suppression or aggregation without distorting data structure.
Examples: Sampling, global recoding, top/bottom coding, local suppression

● Introduction to SDC for Microdata

The purpose of SDC for microdata is to prevent confidential information from being linked to specific respondents when releasing a microdata file. Given an original microdata set V, the goal is to release a protected V′ such that: (1) Disclosure risk is low, and (2) User analyses on V and V′ yield similar results.
The 5-Stage SDC Process for Microdata
StageInputAction / Output
1. Why is confidentiality protection needed?Legal framework, regulations, data collection pledgesJustification document; identifies obligations
2. Key characteristics and uses of dataSurvey design, variable types, intended usersData profile; determines what matters most for utility
3. Disclosure risk definition & assessmentQuasi-identifiers, intruder scenarios, population characteristicsRisk score per record; identification of at-risk combinations
4. Disclosure control methodsRisk assessment results, utility requirementsSelection of SDC methods (perturbative / non-perturbative)
5. ImplementationChosen methods, parameters, softwareProtected microdata file V′; documentation for users

● Disclosure Types in Microdata

Identity Disclosure

A specific individual record can be recognized in a released microdata file.

Achieved by: correct record re-identification by matching a target in sample to an external list of units with identifiers like name and address.
Attribute Disclosure

Sensitive information about a specific individual is revealed through release of the file — even without linking to their name.

Example: All records in a group have the same disease → knowing someone is in that group reveals their disease.
Inferential Disclosure

One can determine the value of some characteristics of an individual more accurately than would otherwise have been possible from the released data.

Example: Estimating income bracket from occupation + neighborhood combination.
Disclosure Scenarios
A disclosure scenario describes: (i) which information is potentially available to an intruder (quasi-identifiers from external archives), and (ii) how such information could be used to identify an individual (matching strategies). Common external archives: voters lists, social surveys, LinkedIn profiles, Facebook data.

● Definitions of Disclosure Risk

A unit is at risk of disclosure when it cannot be confused with several other units. Risk is measured at the record level and aggregated to give a global file-level risk.
Risk for Categorical Quasi-Identifiers
Key Notation:
• K cells C₁,...,CK in the contingency table of quasi-identifiers (the "keys")
• F = (F₁,...,FK) — population frequencies per cell (unknown)
• f = (f₁,...,fK) — sample frequencies per cell (known)
• Risk of re-identification for individual i in cell Ck = 1/Fk

Global Risk Measures:
• τ₁ = (1/n) × Σᵢ (1/Fk(i)) — average individual risk
• Focus on sample uniques (fk = 1) that are also population uniques (Fk = 1) — these are the highest-risk records
Three Approaches to Re-identification Risk
  • Risk based on microdata to be released — Use only information in the sample itself
  • Risk based on population characteristics — Use known or estimated population frequencies
  • Risk based on real external files — Use actual external databases for record linkage
Risk for Continuous Quasi-Identifiers
Outlier-based
Records with extreme values are most at risk — they are uniquely identifiable even after perturbation
Clustering-based
Identify records that form isolated clusters — small or unique clusters are at higher risk
Record Linkage-based
A posteriori approach — attempt actual linking with external file, measure success rate

● Estimating Re-identification Risk

Re-identification occurs when a link is established between a sample unit i and a population unit i* and i* is actually the individual from which i was derived (i.e., the match is correct).
Assumptions Made
  • A sample s from population P is to be released; sampling weights are available
  • The external file covers the whole population P — so the matching unit i* always exists
  • The external file contains individual identifiers AND categorical quasi-identifiers also present in the sample
  • The intruder tries to match sample unit i with population unit i* by comparing quasi-identifier values
  • The intruder has no extra information beyond the external file
  • Units belonging to the same cell Ck are exchangeable for the intruder
Individual Risk: r(i) = 1/Fk(i)    [where Fk = population count in cell of record i]
Global Risk: τ₁ = (1/n) × Σᵢ 1/Fk(i)    [average over all n sample records]
Simplified Global Risk: τ₂ = #{i ∈ s : fk(i)=1 AND Fk(i)=1}    [count of sample+population uniques]

● Non-Perturbative Microdata Masking

Non-perturbative masking does not distort the original data. Instead, it reduces detail through partial suppressions or reductions. The data structure is preserved; only some information is withheld.
MethodHow It WorksExampleKey Property
SamplingPublish only a sample S of original records instead of the full datasetStatistics Catalonia released a 4.06% sample (245,944 of 6M records) of 1991 censusPrivacy by reducing chance of identifying specific individuals; statistical properties preserved if sample is representative

⭐ Exam Tip
Non-perturbative methods do NOT add noise or change values — they suppress or generalize. Perturbative methods change values. This is the fundamental distinction.

● Perturbative Microdata Masking

Perturbative methods allow the entire dataset to be released with altered values rather than suppressed values. An intruder is uncertain whether a match is correct. Most are based on the matrix masking principle.
Matrix Masking: Z = AXB + C
X = original data matrix    A = record-transforming mask    B = variable-transforming mask    C = displacing mask / noise

● Information Loss in Microdata

Information loss is the difference between the utility of original data D and the protected data D′. A protected microdata set is analytically valid if it approximately preserves means, covariance, marginal values for key tabulations, and at least one distributional characteristic.
Information Loss Measures for Continuous Data
Unbounded Measures

Based on Mean Square Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), or Mean Variation between matrices computed on original (X) and protected (X′) data.

Matrices compared: Covariance (V vs V′), Correlation (R vs R′), Principal Component loadings (F vs F′), Communality (C vs C′)

Not bounded in [0,1] — hard to compare across methods.
Bounded Measures

Probability-based, bounded in [0,1].

Convenient for comparing against disclosure risk (also bounded in [0,1]).

Enables explicit optimization: minimize information loss subject to maximum disclosure risk constraint.
MSE = (1/cells) × Σ(Xᵢⱼ − X′ᵢⱼ)²
MAE = (1/cells) × Σ|Xᵢⱼ − X′ᵢⱼ|
Mean Variation = (1/cells) × Σ|(Xᵢⱼ − X′ᵢⱼ)/Xᵢⱼ| × 100%
Information Loss Measures for Categorical Data
Direct Comparison
Categorical distance between original and protected values.
Nominal: dV(c,c′) = 0 if c=c′, else 1
Ordinal: dV(c,c′) = |categories between c and c′| / |D(V)|
Contingency Table Comparison (CTBIL)
Compare contingency tables of original and protected data.
CTBIL = Σ |xsubscripts − x′subscripts| / n
Higher CTBIL = more distortion of variable relationships
Entropy-based (EBIL)
Model protection as noise over information channel.
Uses Shannon's entropy on PRAM Markov matrix.
Measures uncertainty of original value given protected value
⭐ Exam Tip
Key tradeoff: Higher privacy → higher information loss. Optimal SDC = find the method and parameters that minimize information loss while satisfying the disclosure risk threshold. Both measures must be computed and balanced.

● Protecting Explicit Identifiers

EI must be completely masked. Two critical aspects must be addressed: (1) Referential integrity — foreign key relationships in downstream databases must remain valid, and (2) Consistent masking across databases — the same EI in multiple systems must produce the same masked value.
Tokenization for EI Protection
One-Way Tokenization

Generates unique tokens from which the original data cannot be recovered.

Input: 12345 → Token: 40011 (irreversible)

Used for static anonymization — test data, analytics.

Stronger security; token has no relationship to original.
Two-Way Tokenization

Every input is mapped to a unique token. During a transaction, original data can be recovered from the mapping.

Used for dynamic/runtime protection (BPO, credit card industry).

Token database (vault) maintains the mapping securely.

Extensively used in credit card industry — first 5 and last 4 digits preserved (format-preserving).
Substitution: Replace original data with alternative values from an external data source. Example: Names in North American dataset replaced with culturally similar names from a names database. Preserves format and demographic plausibility.
Tokenization vs Randomization:
• Randomization: transformed data are related to original via the randomization function (weaker)
• Tokenization: token has NO relationship to original data (stronger)
• Prior knowledge of randomization method helps identify original data; tokenization is immune to this
• Additive noise: has similar statistical properties to original; a well-designed token does NOT

● Protecting Quasi-Identifiers

Masking EI alone is insufficient. An adversary can still use QI for record linkage — linking a record in the anonymized database to a record in an external data source (voters list, social network). QI attributes are categorical nominal — no arithmetic operations are possible.

● Protecting Sensitive Data

SD is typically left in original form for analysis. However, numerical SD provides an easy re-identification avenue for adversaries with background knowledge. In exceptional cases, SD must also be protected.
When SD Must Be Protected:
• Perturbation with additive noise while ensuring mean and covariance of original and perturbed tables remain the same
• This preserves utility (mean and covariance unchanged) while providing privacy (exact values altered)
• Example: Employee salary database — random perturbation applied but statistical properties (mean, σ²) preserved

D′ = T(DQI) — under standard PPDM, only QI is transformed. SD stays unchanged. Under exceptional scenarios (outliers, rare diseases), selective SD protection may be applied.

● k-Anonymity

k-Anonymity is a technique that makes each record of a table identical to at least k–1 other records over a set of QI attributes. It works on the principle of indistinguishability — an adversary cannot identify which of k records corresponds to the target individual.
k-Anonymous: P(identify record owner from QI) ≤ 1/k
Higher k → Higher privacy → Lower utility
Why k-Anonymization?
  • Record linkage prevention — k indistinguishable records means linking probability ≤ 1/k
  • Utility preservation — Non-perturbative generalization preserves truth in data (vs perturbation which destroys it)
  • Outlier protection — Generalization handles outliers that additive noise cannot mask effectively
  • QI–SD correlation preservation — The association between QI and SD must be maintained
Generalization and Suppression
Suppression: Remove a data field from the table entirely. High information loss. Used when a record cannot be generalized sufficiently.
Generalization: Replace more specific values with generic, semantically similar values. Uses Domain Generalization Hierarchy (DGH) and Value Generalization Hierarchy (VGH). Operates at cell, tuple, or attribute level.
Generalization Hierarchies Example
EDUCATION Domain Generalization Hierarchy:
E0: {PhD, Masters, Bachelors, High School}
E1: {Graduate, Undergraduate}          (PhD+Masters → Graduate; Bachelors+HS → Undergraduate)
E2: {Any}                              (Full suppression)

ZIP CODE Domain Generalization Hierarchy:
Z0: {56001, 56002, 56003, 56004}       (exact codes)
Z1: {5600*}                            (first 4 digits)
Z2: {560**}                            (first 3 digits)
Z3: {*****}                            (any)

Node  in the lattice = minimal generalization satisfying k-anonymity
Samarati's Algorithm
AG-TS (Attribute Generalization + Tuple Suppression)
1. Build DGH lattice combining all QI attributes
2. Most specific node = <Z0, E0> (no generalization)
3. Search the lattice using binary search: try h, h/2, h/4,... to find minimal generalization satisfying k-anonymity
4. Select node <Zi, Ej> closest to most specific node that satisfies k-anonymity
Higher lattice nodes = higher generalization = higher privacy but lower utility

❌ Not robust against homogeneity attacks — if all k records have the same SD value, knowing QI reveals SD
❌ Optimal k-anonymization is NP-hard
❌ Inherent problem with high-dimensional data (curse of dimensionality)
❌ No scientific method to determine optimal k — higher k = higher privacy but lower utility
❌ Suppression leads to high information loss; generalization alone leads to highly generic table

● l-Diversity

l-Diversity ensures that each equivalence class (group of k-anonymous records) contains at least l different values for the sensitive attribute. This prevents attribute disclosure even when k-anonymity is satisfied — it addresses the homogeneity attack.
The Homogeneity Attack on k-Anonymity:
A 4-anonymous group where all 4 records have Diagnosis = "Cancer" → knowing someone is in this group immediately reveals their disease. k-anonymity satisfied but attribute disclosure occurs.

l-Diversity Fix: Ensure each group has ≥ l distinct SD values (e.g., l=3: {Cancer, Flu, Diabetes}). An attacker can only narrow down to 1-in-l probability at best.
Drawbacks of l-Diversity
Attack/WeaknessDescriptionExample
Skewness AttackIf global distribution is highly skewed (e.g., 95% Flu, 5% Sinus), a group with {Flu, Sinus} still reveals high probability of Flu95% of population has Flu — l-diverse group {Flu, Sinus} still means ~95% chance target has Flu
Similarity AttackDistinct values may be semantically similar — diversity in values but not in meaningGroup has {Stomach Cancer, Lung Cancer, Breast Cancer} — l=3 but all are "Cancer" semantically

● t-Closeness

t-Closeness requires that the distribution of a sensitive attribute in every equivalence class is close to its distribution in the whole table — the distance must be no more than threshold t.
Belief Model:
• B0 = Prior belief about an individual's sensitive attribute (before seeing any data)
• B1 = Belief after observing the overall dataset distribution Q (global)
• B2 = Belief after observing the equivalence class distribution P (local)

t-Closeness condition: D[P, Q] ≤ t
where D[P, Q] is the Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) between equivalence class distribution P and overall distribution Q

Table satisfies t-closeness if all equivalence classes satisfy t-closeness.
Distance Metrics
Numerical (Ordered) Attributes
Use Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) — the minimum work to transform one distribution into another.

Intuitively: how much "earth" needs to be moved and how far to make the distributions identical.
Categorical Attributes
Equal Distance: all categorical values are equally distant from each other.

Hierarchical Distance: uses domain knowledge taxonomy — distance = path to common parent / height of tree.

● Privacy-Preserving Graph Data

Graph data structures are common in healthcare, finance, electronics, social networks, and more. A graph G(V, E) consists of a set of vertices V and edges E (vertex pairs). Social networks like Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn involve graph data that companies analyze to understand customer behavior.
Structure of Graph Data
Graph ElementDescriptionPrivacy Risk
Vertex PropertiesAttributes of nodes (age, gender, location, preferences)Direct re-identification via EI/QI on nodes
Vertex LabelsUnique identifiers for nodesIdentity disclosure if not anonymized
Link/Edge RelationshipsConnections between nodes (friend, colleague, patient-doctor)Sensitive relationships revealed by edge existence
Graph MetricsDegree centrality, betweenness, closeness, reachability, path lengthStructural fingerprinting — unique graph signature re-identifies nodes
💡 Key Challenge: A graph is a highly interconnected structure. Any change in nodes or edges affects the characteristics and utility of the entire anonymized graph. More dimensions = more attack surfaces than relational data.

● Graph Privacy Model

Identity Protection Methods
Naïve Anonymization
Replace unique node identifier with a random number. Simple, high utility, but low privacy — adversaries with structural knowledge can re-identify using node degree, neighborhood, or graph metrics.
Graph Modification (k-degree anonymous)
For every node v, ensure at least k–1 other nodes share the same degree.

Prevents re-identification by adversaries knowing node degree.

G → G′ by adding/removing edges until degree sequences satisfy k-anonymity.
Clustering
Partition nodes; publish a generalized graph (supernodes + superedges).

Superedges report edge density between partitions.

Sampled graphs from generalized description retain key properties: degree distribution, path lengths, transitivity.
Content Protection
Content (node attributes) in graph data is protected like relational data — using randomization, k-anonymity, l-diversity, and t-closeness applied to vertex attributes.
Link Protection Methods
Naïve Anonymization
Remove sensitive edges Es from edge set E. Simple but links can be re-identified through other edges remaining in the graph.
Random Perturbation
Construct G′ from G through m random edge deletions + m random edge insertions. Vertices unchanged. High privacy (adversary must consider all possible worlds) but high utility cost.
Clustering Method
Cluster nodes by same degree → equivalence classes. Edges connecting ECs are also clustered. Prevents both node and link disclosure through group indistinguishability.
Graph Metrics — Definitions and Formulas
MetricDefinitionFormula
Degree CentralityNumber of edges a node has — indicates importance/influence in networkDC(v) = degree(v) / (n–1) [normalized by max possible degree]
Betweenness CentralityNumber of shortest paths between other node pairs that pass through vBC(v) = Σ_{s≠v≠t} σ(s,t|v) / σ(s,t) [fraction of shortest paths through v]
Closeness CentralitySum of metric distances from v to all its neighboring/reachable nodes — how quickly v can reach othersCC(v) = (n–1) / Σ d(v,u) [multiply by n–1 to normalize]
ReachabilityIn undirected graph: whether a path exists from node a to node bBoolean: R(a,b) = 1 if path exists, 0 otherwise
Effects of Anonymization on Graph Metrics:
Random perturbation (edge insertions/deletions) → changes reachability, closeness, degree centrality
Clustering → changes neighboring nodes → affects betweenness and closeness
Result: Any analysis on modified graph data yields orthogonal (incorrect) results vs original data → utility loss

● Privacy-Preserving Time Series Data

A time series is a sequence of observations indexed by the time of each observation. Examples: stock prices, fund NAV, interest rates, patient health parameters over time, vehicular traffic counts.
Characteristics of Time Series Data
  • Univariate vs Multivariate — Single dependent variable vs multiple variables with respect to time
  • High Dimensionality — 500 time points = 500 dimensions
  • Time Domain and Frequency Domain — Data represented in both domains; frequency domain shows spectrum of frequencies in signal
  • Temporal Evolution — New data stream appended over time; patterns evolve
  • Pattern — Values at consecutive time instants exhibit a pattern (seasonal, cyclical, trend)

● Time Series Data Protection Methods

Time series data classified into EI (masked), QI (anonymized), and SD (left unchanged). Two broad categories of anonymization for the QI component:
1. White Noise Perturbation

Add high-frequency white (random) noise to the original time series.

✅ Good utility — preserves statistical properties, pattern, frequency domain
❌ Weak privacy — easy to attack

Attack: Filtering — white noise is high-frequency; a low-pass filter removes it, recovering near-original values.
Attack: Regression — ineffective when noise is independent.
2. Correlated Noise Perturbation

Add noise correlated with the original time series.

✅ Higher privacy — cannot be removed by simple filtering
❌ Changes both pattern and frequency — affects utility

Attack: Regression — adversary with background knowledge can implement linear regression to recover original values.
Filtering cannot remove correlated noise (no simple separation in frequency domain).
3. k-Anonymization for Time Series
Apply k-anonymization to QI attributes of time series — generalize values so each record is indistinguishable from k–1 others. Challenge: Higher generalization distorts the pattern of the anonymized time series, reducing forecasting accuracy.
Mean of discrete time series: μ = (1/n) × Σ xᵢ
Variance: σ² = (1/n) × Σ(xᵢ − μ)²
Both must be preserved in the anonymized series for valid statistical analysis

● Privacy Preservation of Longitudinal Data

Longitudinal data tracks the same subjects over a period of time, collecting multiple observations at different points. Examples: patient health trajectories, economic shocks on companies, attitude changes over time in social science.
Key Characteristics of Longitudinal Data
  • Clustered structure — Repeated measurements from the same patient form a cluster
  • Records are classified into EI, QI, and SD — same as relational data
  • Strong intra-cluster correlation — Records within a cluster are not independent (unlike multidimensional data)
  • Temporal order within clusters — Pattern reflects response to treatment; must be preserved
  • Records cannot be treated independently — Unlike relational data where each tuple is processed independently
Anonymization Challenges
ChallengeDescription
Identity disclosurePrevent record linkage using QI values across multiple time points
Attribute disclosurePrevent sensitive data linkage (e.g., health measurements reveal condition)
Intra-cluster correlationAny change to values within a cluster breaks the correlation structure
Pattern disruptionThe temporal pattern (treatment response) must not be disturbed — losing pattern = losing clinical utility
Unknown adversary background knowledgeAdversary may know some patient values from prior contact or records
⭐ Key Insight
Longitudinal data = simple structure but complex intra-cluster correlation. Any anonymization must preserve: (1) temporal pattern within cluster, (2) correlation between measurements, and (3) identity/attribute disclosure protection simultaneously.

● Privacy Preservation of Transaction Data

Transaction data are complex records of customer purchases. Used extensively in retail/e-commerce. Each row is a customer; each column is a product (value = 1 if purchased, 0 otherwise). Characterized by high dimensionality (thousands of products) and sparsity (each customer buys very few products).
Structure of Transaction Data
  • EI — Customer ID and Name (not shown in transaction table; removed in pre-processing)
  • QI — Large set of non-sensitive transactions (bread, milk, common items) — very high dimensions
  • SD — Sensitive transactions (expensive products, specific medicines, budget meal plans) — must be protected
  • No fixed length for QI and SD — unlike relational data with fixed schema
Why Transaction Data is Hard to Anonymize
❌ High Dimensionality — Thousands of columns; anonymization methods scale poorly
❌ Sparsity — Each customer's record is unique (very few non-zero entries) → increases re-identification probability
❌ Sparsity reduces background knowledge needed for re-identification (attacker needs fewer known transactions to identify a person)
❌ Difficult privacy-utility balance — Suppressing sensitive transactions directly destroys association rule mining utility
❌ Association mining use case — Bread → Butter, Medicine → Supplement associations are the entire purpose of collecting this data
Privacy Protection Methods for Transaction Data
Generalization / l-Diversity — Applied to fixed schema tables. Result: very high information loss due to sparsity making generalization impractical.
k-Anonymity, l-Diversity, Randomization — Applied to relational representation of transaction tables. Each combination of techniques offers different privacy-utility trade-offs.

Dynamic Data Protection

● Introduction to Dynamic Data Protection

Tokenization is a technique where sensitive data are replaced with a token having the same format as the sensitive data. Used extensively in the Payment Card Industry (PCI). Ideal for protecting sensitive identification information: credit card numbers, SSNs, account numbers.
Anonymization vs Privacy — The Key Distinction
Static Anonymization (at rest)
Focuses on protecting IDENTITY of customer.
Anonymizes EI and QI; leaves SD in original form.
Used for: data mining, analysis, testing.
Dynamic Privacy (at runtime)
Focuses on protecting SD of customer.
Leaves identification data in original form; protects sensitive transactions.
Used for: business operations, real-time transactions.
Critical Insight: Anonymity ≠ Privacy.
Example: An adversary may not know an individual's exact salary, but knowing they are in the "high-income group" is itself a loss of privacy for the individual. Static anonymization alone cannot solve runtime privacy problems.

Types of Tokenization
Dependent Tokenization
Token is generated based on original data (random number, hash, or encrypted value).
The token has some mathematical relationship to the original.
Prior knowledge of tokenization method can help in reverse-engineering.
Weaker than independent tokenization.
Independent Tokenization
Tokens generated entirely independent of original data.
Unique token for every data element; tokens have NO relationship to original.
Token database maintains the mapping (original → token).
Most secure form — like one-way tokenization for static, two-way for runtime.
Credit Card Tokenization Example (16-digit, format-preserving)
Original Credit Card:  4532 0123 4567 8901

Tokenization Process:
  First 6 digits (BIN): 453201  →  preserved (identifies card network/bank)
  Last 4 digits:         8901   →  preserved (customer reference)
  Middle 6 digits:       234567 →  replaced with random token: 408921

Tokenized Result:       4532 0140 8921 8901  ←  same format, different middle digits
                                               ←  token has no mathematical relationship
                                               ←  completely useless outside application
Token Database:         453201_234567_8901  →  408921  (secure mapping)
⭐ Key Point
Tokenization ≠ Additive noise. Additive noise preserves similar statistical properties to the original data. A well-designed token does NOT preserve any statistical properties of the original — this makes it stronger.

● Use Cases for Dynamic Data Protection

Business Process Outsourcing (BPO)

  • Article 6 (Cross-Border Disclosure) — Personal data may be disclosed abroad ONLY if sufficient safeguards (e.g., contractual clauses) ensure adequate protection level. Safeguards must be informed to the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner.
  • Article 7 — Proper authorization and detailed minimum standards for data security
  • Article 10a — Third-party data processing: the instructing party (data controller) is responsible for ensuring compliance and security by the processor

● HIPAA — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996)

HIPAA privacy rule ensures individuals' health information is properly protected while allowing flow of information for high-quality healthcare. Protected Health Information (PHI) = health condition (past/present/future), healthcare provisioned, payment information for healthcare.
Two De-identification Methods
Expert Determination Method
Covered entity removes all direct identifiers AND reduces dimensionality AND restricts distribution through a data use agreement (DUA).

A qualified statistical expert certifies that re-identification risk is very small.
Safe Harbor Method
18 specific attributes must be removed:
Name, Geographic data (zip code, state), Dates (except year), Phone, Fax, Email, SSN, Medical record number, Health plan beneficiary number, Account number, Certificate/license number, Vehicle identifiers, Device identifiers, Web URLs, IP address, Biometric identifiers, Full-face photographs, Any unique identifying number/code

Three Perspectives on HIPAA Anonymization
Record Owner's View
• k-Anonymity on QI — probability 1/k of re-identification
• Risk from external data (social networks, blogs)
• Neighbor/acquaintance background knowledge attack
• Geo-spatial data + health records = complete patient profile for marketing
Business Associate's View
• Illness data relationships with location, ethnicity, age are vital
• Ability to query like original data is important
• Correlation within data must not be altered
• Zip code suppression removes valuable geo-spatial analytical insight
Adversary's View
• Seeks slightest possibility to combine multiple data sources
• Voter list + hospital data = re-identify individuals (Sweeney's Massachusetts governor example)
• Any anomalous record is a high-value target
⭐ HIPAA Anonymization Design Guidelines
1. Correct classification of all attributes (EI, QI, SD, NSD) — context-dependent
2. Value-based anonymization — "EBOLA" and "AIDS" are more identifying than "FLU" — selectively suppress rare diseases
3. Gender-specific illnesses cannot be randomly assigned to different gender rows
4. Date of birth = QI; Date of admission = SD (same attribute category, different classification based on context)

● GDPR — General Data Protection Regulation (EU, 2018)

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), effective 25 May 2018, is the most comprehensive data protection law in the world. It applies to all organizations processing personal data of EU residents, regardless of where the organization is based (extra-territorial reach). It replaced the EU Data Protection Directive 95/46/EC.
7 Core Principles of GDPR (Article 5)
#PrincipleMeaning
1Lawfulness, Fairness & TransparencyData must be processed lawfully (with consent or legitimate basis), fairly, and in a transparent manner
2Purpose LimitationData collected for specified, explicit, legitimate purposes — cannot be used for incompatible purposes
3Data MinimisationCollect only what is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose
4AccuracyPersonal data must be accurate; inaccurate data erased or rectified without delay
5Storage LimitationData kept only as long as necessary for the stated purpose; must define retention periods
6Integrity & ConfidentialityAppropriate security: protection against unauthorised access, accidental loss, destruction, or damage
7AccountabilityController must be able to demonstrate compliance with all principles — document, audit, and prove
Key Rights of Data Subjects
Right of Access
Data subjects can request a copy of their personal data and how it is being used (Article 15).
Right to Erasure ("Right to be Forgotten")
Data subjects can request deletion of personal data in certain circumstances (Article 17).
Right to Data Portability
Data subjects can receive their data in a structured, machine-readable format and transfer it (Article 20).
Right to Rectification
Correct inaccurate or incomplete personal data (Article 16).
Right to Restriction of Processing
Request restriction of processing in specific circumstances (Article 18).
Right to Object
Object to processing for direct marketing or where legitimate interests are cited (Article 21).
Lawful Bases for Processing (Article 6)
Lawful BasisWhen Applicable
ConsentData subject has given freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous consent
ContractProcessing is necessary for performance of a contract with the data subject
Legal ObligationProcessing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation of the controller
Vital InterestsProcessing is necessary to protect someone's life
Public TaskProcessing is necessary for a task carried out in public interest or official authority
Legitimate InterestsProcessing is necessary for legitimate interests of controller, unless overridden by data subject's rights
GDPR vs Previous Directives — Key Improvements
New Rights & Obligations
• Explicit consent requirement (not assumed)
• Right to be forgotten (Article 17)
• Privacy by design and by default
• Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) for high-risk processing
• Data Protection Officer (DPO) mandatory for some organizations
• Breach notification: 72-hour window to notify supervisory authority
Severe Penalties
• Tier 1: Up to €10M or 2% of global annual turnover
• Tier 2: Up to €20M or 4% of global annual turnover (for most serious violations)
• Applies globally — even non-EU organizations must comply if they process EU residents' data
• Extra-territorial scope is the biggest change from Directive 95/46/EC
GDPR and Anonymization
GDPR does NOT apply to truly anonymized data — if data is properly anonymized (irreversible, cannot be linked back to a natural person), it falls outside the scope of GDPR. However, pseudonymized data (tokenized with recoverable mapping) is still considered personal data under GDPR. This distinction is critical for compliance design.
