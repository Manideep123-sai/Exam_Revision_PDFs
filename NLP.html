What is Natural Language Processing?

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a subfield of computer science, information engineering, and artificial intelligence concerned with the interactions between computers and human (natural) languages — in particular, how to program computers to process and analyze large amounts of natural language data.
  • NLP describes the ability of computers to understand human speech as it is spoken
  • It is a branch of AI that helps computers understand, interpret and manipulate human language
  • NLP helps developers organize and structure knowledge to perform tasks like translation, summarization, NER, speech recognition, etc.
  • Machine learning has helped computers parse the ambiguity of human language
  • NLP is a way of computers to analyze, understand and derive meaning from human languages (English, Spanish, Hindi, etc.)
AI: Theory and development of computer systems able to perform tasks normally requiring human intelligence — visual perception, speech recognition, decision making, translation.
ML: A subset of AI that uses statistical techniques to give computers the ability to learn from data, without being explicitly programmed.
💡 NLP Covers Three Human Activities We speak → Speech Recognition (ASR), Spoken Dialogue Systems, Speech-to-Text
We write → Text Generation (NLG), Machine Translation, Auto-complete, Grammar correction
We read → Information Extraction, NER, Text Classification, Question Answering, Summarization

🧩 Components of NLP: NLU & NLG

Natural Language Understanding (NLU)

Helps machines understand and analyze human language by extracting metadata from content — concepts, entities, keywords, emotion, relations, semantic roles.

Used in business applications to understand customer problems in both spoken and written language.

Tasks: mapping input to useful representations, analyzing different aspects of the language.
Natural Language Generation (NLG)

The process of producing meaningful phrases and sentences in the form of natural language from some internal representation.

Three stages:
1. Text Planning — Retrieve relevant content from knowledge base
2. Sentence Planning — Choose words, form phrases, set tone
3. Text Realization — Map sentence plan to sentence structure

Note: NLU is harder than NLG.

🔄 Stages of NLP

Stage Description Example
Morphological Analysis Individual words analyzed into their components (root, affixes, POS) Playing = Play + ing
Syntactic Analysis Linear word sequences transformed into structures showing word relationships Parse tree for a sentence
Semantic Analysis Transformation from input text to internal representation that reflects meaning Understanding "bank" as financial vs river
Discourse Analysis Resolving references between sentences; coherence, anaphora, topic flow "Ravi bought a book. He read it." → he=Ravi, it=book
Pragmatic Analysis Reinterpreting what was said to what was actually meant Context and speaker intention
⚠️ Exam Tip: Order of Stages Morphological → Syntactic → Semantic → Discourse → Pragmatic. Think: M-S-S-D-P (My Syntax Semantics Discourse Pragmatics).
Discourse Example: "Ravi bought a book. He read it yesterday." — Understanding he → Ravi and it → book is a discourse-level phenomenon (anaphora resolution).
Pragmatics: Focuses on how context, speaker intention, background knowledge, and real-world reasoning affect the meaning of a single utterance. Interprets the hidden meaning behind one utterance using real-world knowledge.

⚡ Ambiguities in NLP

Ambiguity is one of the core challenges of NLP. The same word, phrase, or sentence can have multiple valid interpretations.
Lexical Ambiguity

A word has multiple meanings (same form, different senses).

• "Look" — verb (He gave me a warm look) or noun (Look at the stars!)
• "Silver" — noun, adjective, or verb
• "Park" — verb (to park the car) or noun (a park near the beach)
• "Bank" — financial institution or river bank
Syntactic Ambiguity

A sentence can be parsed in two or more distinct structural ways.

• "The chicken is ready to eat." — Is the chicken cooked, or is it hungry?
• "I saw the man with the telescope." — Did I use a telescope, or did the man have one?
• "Time flies like an arrow" — multiple parse trees!
Why NLP is Hard: Non-standard English (u 2 #neversaynever), idioms (dark horse, get cold feet), neologisms (unfriend, retweet), segmentation issues, tricky entity names (A Bug's Life), world knowledge requirements.

Word Analogy Example (How NLP Works)

NLP draws on contextual patterns, not just rules.

• Man → Woman as King → ? → Queen (gender/role analogy)
   Meaning(king) – Meaning(man) + Meaning(woman) = queen
• King → Kings as Queen → ? → Queens (morphology analogy, singular → plural)

✂️ Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of breaking text into individual units called tokens (words, subwords, or characters). It is the first step in almost all NLP pipelines.

Types of Tokenization Challenges

Punctuation Ambiguity

Quotation marks & Apostrophes:
• Genitive case: Peter's bag
• Contractions: I'm, won't (expand with language knowledge)
• Plurals: M.D.'s, D.O.'s
• Possessives: llama's, children's
Apostrophes ≠ Single quotes. Apostrophes denote possessive form or omission. Single quotes indicate a quotation within a quotation.
Multi-part Words (Agglutinations)

• Compound words: hyphenated (English) vs not (German)
• German: Nachkriegszeit, Nichtraucher (single token)
• French: va-t-il (will it?), c'est-à-dire (that is)
• Delhi-based → multi-token word
• end-of-line → single token word
Contractions should be expanded. Hyphen usage varies by language.
Multi-Word Expressions: "In spite of" = Despite (one semantic meaning). Loaned words: au pair, de facto. Dates can appear in many formats: November 18, 1989 = Nov. 18, 1989 = 11/18/89 — should be treated as single tokens. Numbers like $3.9 to $4 million.

Tokenization in Unsegmented Languages (CJK + Thai)

Languages without word spaces: Chinese, Japanese, Korean (CJK), Thai, Balinese, Javanese, Khmer.
Common approaches:
1. Extensive Word List — but no widely accepted definition of "word"
2. Maximum Matching Algorithm (Greedy) — find longest word starting at current character
3. FMM (Forward Maximum Matching — Left to Right)
4. BMM (Backward Maximum Matching — Right to Left)
5. BiMM (Bidirectional) — compares FMM & BMM, chooses better output using linguistic criteria
Greedy Algorithm Failure in English (Why BiMM is Needed)
Input: "thetabledownthere"  (remove spaces from "the table down there")

FMM result:  theta | bled | own | there   ← WRONG!
BMM result:  the | table | down | there   ← CORRECT!

BiMM compares both outputs and picks the linguistically better one.
Chinese Segmentation (Hanzi): Thousands of characters; words = 1 or more. Approaches: Statistical (mutual information), Lexical rule-based (syntax/semantics), Hybrid (weighted FST to identify dictionary entries).
Japanese Segmentation: Uses Kanji + Hiragana + Katakana + Romanji. Challenges: identifying base, mix of writing systems. Statistical techniques similar to Chinese apply.

📄 Sentence Segmentation

Sentence Segmentation (Sentence Boundary Detection) is the task of finding where one sentence ends and the next begins.
  • ! and ? are relatively unambiguous sentence terminators
  • Period "." is highly ambiguous: sentence boundary, abbreviation (Inc., Dr.), decimal (0.02%, 4.3)
  • In most NLP applications, a sentence begins with a capital letter and ends with a period/question/exclamation
  • A semicolon can also connect two sentences with the same meaning as a period
Solution: Binary Classifier on "."
Build a classifier that looks at each "." and decides: EndOfSentence / NotEndOfSentence
Classifier types: hand-written rules, regular expressions, or machine learning models.
Features: word before ".", word after ".", is it an abbreviation?, capitalization of next word?
⚠️ Thai Sentence Segmentation Thai doesn't use periods. Spaces are used like commas to mark clauses. Carriage returns can be mixed with spaces — requires special handling.

🔬 Introduction to Morphology

Morphology is the branch of linguistics that studies the internal structure of words and how words are formed from smaller meaningful units.

In NLP: Morphology explains how words are built and how their forms change. This is critical for stemming, lemmatization, POS tagging, and word generation.
Morphology vs Syntax

Morphology = study of the structure of words
Syntax = study of the structure of sentences

Together they help linguists understand how language works.
Why Morphology Matters in NLP

play, plays, played, playing — all from the same base word "play" but convey different meanings/grammatical roles.

Helps identify word meanings, recognize grammatical info, analyze word variations.

Three Dimensions of Morphology

Dimension Question NLP Application
Morpheme Count How many morphemes? Text analysis, tokenization
Morphological Process What functions do morphemes perform? Stemming, Lemmatization, POS tagging
Morpheme Combination How do morphemes combine? Morphological parsing, word generation

Word Complexity by Language Type

Isolating Languages (e.g., Mandarin Chinese)
No inflections — words are single morphemes. Context determines meaning (tense, number, etc.).

Gou bu ai chi qingcai = "dog not like eat vegetable" (can be present/past, singular/plural based on context)
Agglutinative Languages (e.g., Turkish, Finnish, Tamil)
Words built by attaching multiple morphemes, each encoding one piece of info.

ev-ler-im-den = house+plural+my+from = "from my houses"
Inflectional Languages (e.g., Latin)
A single bound morpheme simultaneously conveys multiple pieces of linguistic information (case, number, gender, tense).

🧱 Morphemes & Types

A morpheme is the minimum word-bearing unit — the smallest meaningful unit of language.
Fox = 1 morpheme (Fox) | Cats = 2 morphemes (Cat + s)
Free Morphemes
Can occur as words by themselves.
Examples: go, book, eat, date, weak

They carry the core meaning and can stand alone without modification.
Bound Morphemes
Cannot stand alone — must be attached to a free morpheme.
Examples: -ing, -s, -ness, -ed, -er, un-, re-, pre-

Also called affixes: prefix (before stem), suffix (after), infix (inside), circumfix (both ends).
Stems vs Affixes:
• Stems (Roots): Core meaning-bearing units — the main morpheme of the word
• Affixes: Bits and pieces that adhere to stems to change meanings and grammatical functions
• Prefix: Incomplete = In+complete | Suffix: Browser = Browse+er | Circumfix: Prefix + Suffix together
Word Formation Chains
compute → computer → computerize → computerization
compute → computation → computational
compute → computerize → computerizable

🔄 Inflectional Morphology

Inflectional Morphology: Combination of a word stem with a grammatical morpheme that keeps it in the same word class (same POS).
Purpose: Express grammatical relationships — tense, number, person, case.
  • English has a relatively simple inflectional system — nouns, verbs, and adjectives can be inflected
  • Regular: Walk → walks/walking/walked (can be generated by general rules)
  • Irregular: Eat → ate/eaten, Catch → caught, Cut → cut/cut, Goose → geese
  • English noun inflections: plural (-s/-es/-ies/irregular) and possessive ('s)
Type Example Rule
Regular Plural cat → cats, box → boxes, butterfly → butterflies +s / +es / -y → ies
Irregular Plural goose → geese, ox → oxen, mouse → mice No rule — memorize
Possessive llama's, children's, Euripides' comedies +'s or ' alone
3rd person singular verb I run → John runs +s marks 3rd person singular
Past tense regular walk → walked +ed
Past tense irregular go → went, catch → caught No rule
⚠️ Exam Tip Inflectional morphology: same word class (verb stays verb, noun stays noun). -s on noun = plural, -s on verb = 3rd person singular. -ed = past tense. Irregular verbs: eat/ate/eaten, catch/caught, cut/cut.

🔧 Derivational Morphology

Derivational Morphology: Produces a NEW word, usually with a different part-of-speech category. The new word is derived from the old word.
Noun → Verb Examples:
Critic → Criticize (-ize)
Organ → Organize (-ize)
Standard → Standardize (-ize)
Product → Produce (changes stem)
Regulation → Regulate (-ate)
Verb → Noun Examples:
Establish → Establishment (-ment)
Sing → Singer (-er)
Employ → Employee (-ee)
Create → Creation (-tion)
Inflectional Derivational
Changes POS? No (stays same class) Yes (usually)
Productivity More regular/productive Less productive
Example run → runs (verb stays verb) establish → establishment (verb → noun)
NLP Use POS tagging, stemming Morphological parsing, word generation

✂️ Stemming & Lemmatization

Stemming is the process of reducing a word to its stem/root form, often by chopping off suffixes. Results may not be a real word.

• "playing" → "play"
• "studies" → "studi"
• "running" → "run"

Common algorithm: Porter Stemmer
Fast but imprecise — may over-stem (studies → studi) or under-stem.
Lemmatization is the process of reducing a word to its canonical base form (lemma) using vocabulary and morphological analysis. Always returns a valid word.

• "better" → "good"
• "am/is/are" → "be"
• "studies" → "study"

Uses a dictionary/WordNet. More accurate but slower.
Requires knowing the POS to lemmatize correctly.
Word Stemming Result Lemmatization Result
studies studi study
running run run
better better good
went went go
are are be
💡 When to Use Which? Use stemming when speed matters and slight inaccuracy is acceptable (information retrieval, search engines). Use lemmatization when accuracy is important (chatbots, text analytics, NLU).

🏷️ Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging

POS Tagging is the process of assigning each word in a sentence its part-of-speech category (noun, verb, adjective, etc.) based on its definition and its context.
Penn Treebank POS Tags (Common)
NN  = Noun (singular)           NNS = Noun (plural)
NNP = Proper Noun (singular)    NNPS = Proper Noun (plural)
VB  = Verb (base form)          VBD = Verb (past tense)
VBZ = Verb (3rd sing. present)  VBG = Verb (gerund/participle)
JJ  = Adjective                 RB  = Adverb
DT  = Determiner                IN  = Preposition/Subordinating conjunction
CC  = Coordinating conjunction  PRP = Personal pronoun
TO  = "to"

Example: John/NNP saw/VBD the/DT cat/NN
Ambiguity Example: "John saw the saw and decided to take it to the table."
The word "saw" can be NNP (proper noun), VBD (past verb), or NN (noun). "Table" can be NN or VB.
Forward classification and backward classification can give different results — the full sentence context is needed.

Approaches to POS Tagging

Rule-based: Hand-written rules. E.g., "if word ends in -ing and follows 'is', tag as VBG"
Statistical (ML): Trained on labeled corpora. Forward/Backward classification using features.
Probabilistic Sequence Models: HMM (generative) and CRF (discriminative) — consider full sequence context.
⚠️ Key Problem: "Time flies like an arrow" Is "flies" a noun or verb? Is "like" a verb, preposition, or conjunction? Syntax alone cannot resolve this — semantic and pragmatic knowledge are required. This is why probabilistic sequence models (HMM, CRF) are preferred over simple classifiers.

🎲 Hidden Markov Model (HMM)

HMM is a probabilistic generative model for sequences. In POS tagging: hidden states = POS tags, observations = words. The model generates words from tags.

Formal Definition

Components of an HMM:

• N states: S = {s₀, s₁, s₂, ..., sₙ, sF} (start s₀, end sF)
• M observations: V = {v₁, v₂, ..., vM} (vocabulary)
• Initial state probabilities: π(sᵢ) — probability of starting at state sᵢ
• State Transition Matrix A: aᵢⱼ = P(state j at t+1 | state i at t)
• Observation/Emission Probability Matrix B: bᵢ(oₖ) = P(oₖ | state i)
• Total parameter set: λ = {A, B, π}

Three Fundamental Tasks

Task Problem Algorithm
Learning Given training data, determine A, B, π Forward-Backward (Baum-Welch)
Likelihood Given HMM λ and observation O, find P(O|λ) Forward Algorithm — O(TN²)
Decoding Given HMM λ and O, find most likely state sequence Viterbi Algorithm
Bayes' Theorem for POS Tagging:
We want: argmax_T P(T|W) = argmax_T [P(W|T) × P(T)] / P(W)
Since P(W) is constant for all tags, compare: P(W|T) × P(T)
• P(W|T) = emission probability (how likely the word given the tag)
• P(T) = tag transition probability
Forward Algorithm (Observation Likelihood):
Uses dynamic programming. Exploits the Markov assumption: probability of being in any state at time t only depends on state at t-1.
Computes a forward trellis — O(TN²) vs naive O(TN^T).

Viterbi Algorithm (Decoding):
Finds the most likely sequence of hidden states for a given observation sequence. Uses dynamic programming on the same trellis structure.
💡 HMM Generation Procedure 1. Set initial state q₁ = s₀
2. For t = 1 to T: Transit to qt+1 = sⱼ based on transition A; pick observation oₜ = vₖ based on emission B
HMM generates word sequences from tag sequences (generative model).
Generative Model (HMM): Captures joint probability P(X, Y). Can generate new data instances. Models the full data distribution. Less discriminative power.
Discriminative Model (CRF): Captures conditional probability P(Y|X). Directly discriminates between different classes. Better for classification tasks like POS tagging.

📐 Context-Free Grammar (CFG)

A Context-Free Grammar (CFG) is a formal grammar used to describe the syntactic structure of language. It consists of production rules that define how sentences are structured.
CFG Rules & Parse Tree
S  → NP VP          (Sentence = Noun Phrase + Verb Phrase)
NP → Det N          (Noun Phrase = Determiner + Noun)
VP → V NP | V PP    (Verb Phrase = Verb + NP or Verb + PrepP)
PP → P NP           (Prepositional Phrase = Preposition + NP)
Det → the | a
N  → dog | cat | mat
V  → sat | saw
P  → on

Parse of "The dog sat on the mat":
         S
       /   \
      NP    VP
     / \   /  \
   Det  N  V    PP
   the dog sat  / \
              P   NP
             on  / \
               Det  N
               the  mat
Grammar Type Rules NLP Use
Regular Grammar A → aB or A → a Lexical analysis, tokenization
Context-Free Grammar (CFG) A → α (any string of terminals/nonterminals) Syntactic parsing, constituency analysis
Probabilistic CFG (PCFG) CFG + probability for each rule Disambiguation of parse trees

🌲 Constituency Parsing

Constituency Parsing (phrase structure parsing) analyzes sentences by identifying constituents (phrases) — groups of words that function as a single unit. Based on CFG rules.
  • NP (Noun Phrase), VP (Verb Phrase), PP (Prepositional Phrase), AP (Adjective Phrase)
  • The parse tree shows the hierarchical phrase structure
  • Ambiguous sentences have multiple valid parse trees — PCFG assigns probabilities to choose the most likely
  • The CYK (Cocke-Younger-Kasami) algorithm is used for efficient constituency parsing
⚠️ Structural Ambiguity "I saw the man with the telescope" has two parse trees:
(1) [VP saw [NP the man with the telescope]] — man has telescope
(2) [VP saw [NP the man] [PP with the telescope]] — I used telescope
PCFG resolves by assigning higher probability to the more common structure.

🔗 Dependency Parsing

Dependency Parsing analyzes the grammatical structure of a sentence by establishing relationships (dependencies) between words. Each word has a HEAD (the word it depends on) and a DEPENDENCY RELATION.
Dependency Tree: "She writes a letter"
       writes (ROOT)
      /       \
    She        letter
   (nsubj)    (dobj)
               /
              a
            (det)

Token  | Dep Relation | Head
-------|--------------|------
She    | nsubj        | writes
writes | ROOT         | writes
a      | det          | letter
letter | dobj         | writes
  • The verb is the root — it is the central element around which all other words are organized
  • The verb determines subject-verb agreement and governs object/complement relationships
  • Common dependency relations: nsubj (nominal subject), dobj (direct object), det (determiner), prep (preposition), pobj (prepositional object), amod (adjectival modifier)
  • Tools: spaCy, Stanford NLP, NLTK
  • In complex sentences, each clause may have its own root verb
Constituency vs Dependency
Constituency: groups words into hierarchical phrases
Dependency: captures direct word-to-word relationships
Dependency is more direct for semantic analysis
ADP = Adposition (Preposition/Postposition)
DET = Determiner
NOUN = Noun
VERB = Verb
ADJ = Adjective
ADV = Adverb

⚙️ Transition-based Parsing

Transition-based dependency parsing builds the dependency tree incrementally by processing words one-by-one and applying a sequence of transitions. Known for efficiency.

Data Structures

Stack: Holds partially parsed words
Buffer: Contains remaining unprocessed words
Parse Tree: Final structure being built
Transitions:
• Shift: Move next word from buffer to stack
• Left Arc: Create dependency from top to below-top, remove top
• Right Arc: Create dependency from below-top to top, remove top
Example: "John eats an apple"
Step | Action     | Stack          | Buffer             | Dependencies
-----|------------|----------------|--------------------|-------------
  0  | Init       | []             | [John, eats, an, apple] | {}
  1  | Shift      | [John]         | [eats, an, apple]  | {}
  2  | Shift      | [John, eats]   | [an, apple]        | {}
  3  | Right Arc  | [eats]         | [an, apple]        | {eats → John (nsubj)}
  4  | Shift      | [eats, an]     | [apple]            | ...
  5  | Shift      | [eats, an, apple] | []             | ...
  6  | Left Arc   | [eats, apple]  | []                 | {apple ← an (det)}
  7  | Right Arc  | [eats]         | []                 | {eats → apple (dobj)}
⚠️ Termination Condition Parsing terminates when the buffer is empty AND the stack contains only the root node of the parse tree.

💡 Semantic Analysis

Semantic Analysis studies the meaning of words and sentences. Three types of analysis help understand sentence meaning.
Semantic Features

Breaking words into meaning components → useful for categorization, restrictions, disambiguation.

girl → [+Human, +Young, +Female]
dog → [-Human, +Animal]
Lexical Relations

How words relate to each other: synonymy, antonymy, hypernymy, hyponymy, meronymy, polysemy, homonymy.
Semantic/Thematic Roles

Identifying the roles words play in the situation described (Agent, Patient, Theme, etc.).

Discourse & World Knowledge

Discourse: The prior context provides references for pronouns. Temporal sequences require discourse knowledge.
"Mary was hired. She untangled it." → understanding the roles requires knowing what "she" and "it" refer to.
World Knowledge: Allows understanding conversations beyond the literal text — knowledge about jobs, office politics, structure of arguments, etc. Without world knowledge, many NLP tasks fail.

🎭 Thematic Roles

Thematic Roles (Semantic Roles / Theta Roles) identify the underlying semantic relationship between a verb and its arguments — who did what to whom, what was affected, how something was experienced.
Role Definition Example
Agent Initiator of action, acts with volition Jack ate the beans.
Patient Entity undergoing effect, often changes state Sue mowed the lawn.
Theme Entity moved by action or whose location is described Fred threw the rock.
Experiencer Entity aware of action/state but not in control Kim saw the deer.
Beneficiary Entity for whose benefit action was performed Mary studied hard for her mother.
Instrument Means by which action is performed Fred opened the lock with a paper clip.
Location Place in which something is situated or takes place The picture hangs above the fireplace.
Goal Entity towards which something moves Lee walked to school.
Source Entity from which something moves Sue ran from the policeman.
Recipient Subtype of Goal involved in changes of possession Bill sold the car to Mary.
💡 Theme vs Patient All Patients are Themes, but not all Themes are Patients.
Theme = general role (entity involved in situation)
Patient = special case of Theme (entity that changes state)

🔍 Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)

WSD is the task of determining which meaning (sense) of a word is being used in a particular context. Critical because many words have multiple meanings (polysemy) or refer to different concepts (homonymy).
Example: "The bank will not be accepting cash on Saturdays" (financial bank) vs "The river overflowed the bank" (river bank). The word "bass" can mean a musical instrument, a low-frequency sound, or a type of fish.

Approaches to WSD

Knowledge-based WSD

Uses a lexical knowledge base (e.g., WordNet).

Lesk Algorithm: Compare context words with glosses (definitions) of each sense. Pick the sense whose gloss has maximum overlap with context.

Code: from nltk.wsd import lesk; sense = lesk(sentence, 'bank')
Supervised WSD

Train a classifier on sense-annotated corpora.

Features: surrounding words, POS tags, syntactic relations.

SemCor is a commonly used sense-annotated corpus. Requires manually labeled training data for each word.
💡 Applications of WSD Information Retrieval (better search), Machine Translation (correct word choice), Text Analysis (accurate sentiment, summarization). WSD is a hard problem — even humans sometimes disagree on senses.

📚 WordNet & Lexical Relations

WordNet is a large lexical database of English. Words are grouped into sets of synonyms called synsets, each representing a distinct concept. Used extensively in WSD.
Lexical Relation Definition Example
Homonymy Same form, completely different meanings (different origins) Bank (financial) vs Bank (river)
Polysemy Same word with multiple related meanings "Head" — body part, leader, foam on beer
Synonymy Different words with same/similar meaning sofa / couch, big / large
Antonymy Words with opposite meanings hot / cold, up / down
Hypernymy Broad category word (supertype) colour is hypernym of red; vehicle is hypernym of car
Hyponymy Specific instance word (subtype) red is hyponym of colour; car is hyponym of vehicle
Meronymy Part-whole relationship (part of) Carburetor is meronym of engine; fingers are meronyms of hand

🔗 Coreference Resolution

Coreference Resolution is the task of identifying all expressions (mentions) in a text that refer to the same entity.
"Victoria Chen works at Megabucks Banking. She was promoted." → "She" refers to "Victoria Chen".

Mention Detection

A mention is any expression that could refer to an entity.
Types: Proper noun mentions (John, New York), Pronoun mentions (he, she, it), Noun phrase mentions (the tall man), Nominal mentions (the teacher, a student).
Challenge: Over-generating mentions leads to false positives; under-generating misses coreference links.

Mention-Pair Models

Traditional Mention-Pair Architecture:
• Treats coreference as binary classification: for each pair of mentions (m_i, m_j), predict coreferent / not coreferent
• Features: gender/number agreement, syntactic role, semantic compatibility
• Training: closest antecedent = positive, all mentions in between = negative
• Closest-first clustering: run right-to-left, first antecedent with P > 0.5 is linked
• Best-first clustering: all i-1 antecedents evaluated, most probable is chosen
Advantage of Mention-Pair
Simple to implement and understand. Binary classification is well-studied.
Disadvantages of Mention-Pair
• Doesn't directly compare candidate antecedents
• Ignores discourse model (looks only at mention pairs, not entities)
• Makes decisions locally without global consistency

Example problem: "Jack met John. He greeted him." → model may assign both "He" and "him" to both Jack and John, violating global consistency.
💡 Modern Approaches Neural end-to-end coreference models (Lee et al. 2017 with BERT) jointly detect mentions and resolve coreference. Much more accurate than mention-pair models.

Language Models
N-Grams, Perplexity, Smoothing & TF-IDF

📊 N-Gram Language Models

A Language Model (LM) estimates the probability of a sequence of words: P(w₁, w₂, ..., wₙ). Predicts the probability of the next word given the previous words.
N-gram Models: Approximate the probability of a word given all previous words using only the previous (n-1) words — the Markov assumption.

Unigram: P(w₁w₂...wₙ) ≈ ∏ P(wᵢ)
Bigram: P(wᵢ | w₁...wᵢ₋₁) ≈ P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁)
Trigram: P(wᵢ | w₁...wᵢ₋₁) ≈ P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₂, wᵢ₋₁)

MLE (Maximum Likelihood Estimate) for bigrams:
P(wᵢ | wᵢ₋₁) = C(wᵢ₋₁, wᵢ) / C(wᵢ₋₁)
N-gram Type Context Used Example
Unigram (1-gram) No context P(cat) = 0.02
Bigram (2-gram) Previous 1 word P(cat|the) = 0.15
Trigram (3-gram) Previous 2 words P(cat|chased, the) = 0.08
4-gram, 5-gram Previous 3, 4 words More context, more data needed
  • Applications: Autocomplete, Machine Translation, Question Answering, Text Summarization, Text Generation, Speech Recognition
  • Limitation: Traditional LMs use only left-to-right context (unidirectional)
  • Higher n = more context but needs exponentially more data; lower n = less data but less context

📐 Perplexity

Perplexity (PP) is an intrinsic evaluation metric for language models. A lower perplexity = better model (more certain predictions).
Formula: PP(W) = P(w₁w₂...wₙ)^(-1/N)

Where N = number of tokens (consider only </s> for sentence endings in counting)

Example Calculation (Bigram):
Sentences: <s> Who am I </s> | <s> I would like to know </s> | <s> I am not </s>
Query: "I I am not"
P = P(I|<s>) × P(I|I) × P(am|I) × P(not|am) × P(</s>|not)
P = ¾ × 1/5 × 2/5 × 1/3 × 1/1 = 0.02
PP = (0.02)^(-1/5) = 2.186
Intrinsic Evaluation
Measure perplexity on held-out test set. Fast and easy. Good for comparing models during development.
Extrinsic Evaluation
Plug model into real application (e.g., speech recognizer) and measure end-to-end performance. Time consuming (days) but the "true" evaluation. Perplexity is only a proxy.
⚠️ Important Perplexity is only useful if test data looks like training data. It's a pilot experiment metric — generally not sufficient to publish. For real evaluation, use extrinsic/in-vivo evaluation.

🌀 Smoothing Techniques

Smoothing is applied to handle the zero probability problem — n-grams not seen in training data get P=0, which breaks language models. Smoothing adjusts probability estimates to handle unseen events.
Shakespeare produced 300,000 bigram types out of V² = 844 million possible bigrams — 99.96% of possible bigrams were never seen! Without smoothing, any sentence with an unseen bigram gets P = 0.

Types of Smoothing

Method Idea Formula
Add-One (Laplace) Add 1 to all n-gram counts before normalizing P(z|xy) = C(xyz)+1 / C(xy)+V
Add-k (Delta) Add fraction k (0 < k < 1) instead of 1 P(z|xy) = C(xyz)+k / C(xy)+kV
Backoff If trigram unseen, back off to bigram; if bigram unseen, back off to unigram Use lower-order n-gram with discounting
Kneser-Ney Most sophisticated — uses absolute discounting + interpolation based on word diversity Best performing in practice
⚠️ Add-One Smoothing Problem Add-one smoothing makes very large changes to counts. C(want|to) changed from 608 to 238. P(to|want) decreases from 0.66 to 0.26. This shows add-one redistributes too much probability mass to unseen events. Kneser-Ney is preferred in practice.

📈 TF-IDF

TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) is a numerical statistic that reflects how important a word is to a document in a collection (corpus). Used for information retrieval and text mining.
TF (Term Frequency):
How often a term appears in a document.

TF(t, d) = count of t in d / total terms in d

High TF = word appears often in this document
IDF (Inverse Document Frequency):
How rare a term is across all documents.

IDF(t) = log(N / df(t))
where N = total docs, df(t) = docs containing t

High IDF = rare word (more discriminative)
TF-IDF Formula: TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t)

Example with 3 documents:
D1: "cat sat mat" | D2: "dog sat log" | D3: "cat chased dog"
Vocabulary: {cat, sat, mat, dog, log, chased}
TF(cat, D1) = 1/3 = 0.33 | IDF(cat) = log(3/2) = 0.176
TF-IDF(cat, D1) = 0.33 × 0.176 ≈ 0.058
Common words like "sat" get lower TF-IDF (appear in multiple docs = lower IDF).
💡 TF-IDF in NLP Used in: information retrieval (search engines), text classification, extractive summarization (TF-IDF sentence scoring), keyword extraction. Stop words (the, a, is) get very low IDF since they appear in all documents.

Deep Learning & NLP
Transformer Architecture, BERT, Text Classification, Neural MT

⚡ Transformer Architecture

The Transformer is a deep learning architecture based entirely on the self-attention mechanism, proposed by Vaswani et al. in 2017 — "Attention Is All You Need" (NeurIPS 2017).

Why Transformers? Limitations of RNNs/LSTMs

RNN/LSTM Limitation Transformer Solution
Sequential processing — cannot parallelize Processes entire sequence in parallel
Long-range dependency issues Self-attention directly connects all word pairs
Poor GPU utilization Scales extremely well on GPUs/TPUs
Vanishing/exploding gradients (BPTT) Residual connections + layer normalization
Memory bottleneck (single hidden state) Rich multi-head attention representations
Self-Attention Mechanism:
Each word attends to every other word and assigns importance weights.

Three learned matrices per word:
• Query (Q): What am I looking for?
• Key (K): What do I contain?
• Value (V): What information do I pass?

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ) × V

Example: "The cat chased the mouse because it was hungry."
→ Self-attention assigns higher importance to "cat" when processing "it"

Multi-Head Attention

Multi-Head Attention runs multiple attention operations in parallel, each "head" focuses on different aspects of the sentence (syntax, semantics, long-range dependencies).

Example: "The bank raised interest rates, and the river bank flooded." Different attention heads help BERT understand the two different meanings of "bank" in context.

Transformer Components

  • Positional Encoding: Since transformers have no built-in notion of word order (unlike RNNs), positional encoding is added to input embeddings
  • Layer Normalization: Ensures layers remain in reasonable range (training stability)
  • Residual Connections: Skip connections that prevent vanishing gradients
  • Feed-Forward Networks: Position-wise FFN after each attention layer
  • Label Smoothing: Insert some uncertainty in the training process
  • Specialized Training Schedule: Adjust learning rate of Adam optimizer
Transformer Block
Input Embeddings + Positional Encoding
          ↓
    [Multi-Head Attention]
          ↓ (+ Residual)
    [Layer Normalization]
          ↓
    [Feed-Forward Network]
          ↓ (+ Residual)
    [Layer Normalization]
          ↓
    Output Representations

🤖 BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

BERT is a pre-trained deep learning model developed by Google in 2018. It uses only the Encoder part of the Transformer (NLU-oriented). BERT currently powers Google Search.
BERT Base:
• 12 Transformer layers
• 768 hidden size
• 12 attention heads
• 110M parameters
BERT Large:
• 24 Transformer layers
• 1024 hidden size
• 16 attention heads
• 340M parameters

Bidirectional Approach

Previous models were unidirectional:
• GPT: Left-to-right only
• ELMo: Two separate LSTMs (shallow bidirectionality)

BERT is truly bidirectional — processes context from BOTH left and right simultaneously.

Example: "He went to the bank to deposit money" vs "She sat by the bank of the river."
BERT understands the correct meaning of "bank" in both sentences by processing words on both sides.
BERT is Good At:
Sentiment analysis, Question answering, Named Entity Recognition, Sentence similarity, Text classification
BERT is NOT Designed For:
Text generation, Machine translation (without decoder), Autoregressive sequence generation

Static vs Contextual Embeddings

Static (Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText): Each word has ONE fixed vector regardless of context.
Contextual (BERT): Same word gets DIFFERENT vectors depending on context — "bank" in financial context vs river context gets different representations.

🎓 BERT Pre-training & Input Representation

BERT is pre-trained on large unlabeled text (BooksCorpus + Wikipedia) using two unsupervised tasks, then fine-tuned on specific tasks with labeled data.
Pre-training Task 1: Masked Language Model (MLM)

Randomly mask 15% of tokens in input. Train model to predict masked tokens from context (both left and right).

"The [MASK] sat on the mat." → predict "cat"

This forces the model to learn bidirectional context.
Pre-training Task 2: Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)

Given two sentences A and B, predict: Is B the actual next sentence after A, or a random sentence?

Helps BERT understand sentence relationships — critical for QA, NLI tasks.

Input Representation

BERT input = Token Embedding + Position Embedding + Segment Embedding

Special tokens:
• [CLS]: Added at start — its final output vector represents the whole sentence (used for classification)
• [SEP]: Separates two sentences

Example:
Input: "India wins the world cup!"
After tokenization: [CLS] india wins the world cup final in thrilling fashion ! [SEP]
Each token gets a 768-dimensional embedding.

Fine-tuning Workflow

  1. Pre-training: BERT learns general language patterns from massive unlabeled corpora
  2. Add task head: Add a small classification/regression layer on top of [CLS] token output
  3. Fine-tune: Train on small labeled dataset for the specific downstream task (sentiment, NER, QA)
  4. Inference: Feed new text through BERT + task head → predicted label

🏷️ Text Categorization / Classification

Text Categorization is the process of automatically assigning predefined categories or tags to text based on its content. Converts raw text into structured labels that computers can act on.
  • Gmail classifies emails into "Primary", "Promotions", or "Spam"
  • Netflix tags shows under "Comedy" or "Drama"
  • News articles classified as "Politics" or "Sports"
BERT for Text Classification:
1. Feed text: [CLS] india wins the world cup ! [SEP]
2. BERT produces 768-dim vector for [CLS] token representing full text meaning
3. Pass [CLS] vector through linear layer → softmax → class probabilities
4. Output: Sports: 3.90, Politics: -0.75 → Sports (highest logit)

Self-attention captures relationships between all words. Positional embeddings preserve word order. Multiple attention heads capture syntax and semantics.
⚠️ Text Processing Before Classification Raw text → Tokenization → Lowercasing/normalization → Stop word removal → Stemming/lemmatization → POS tagging → Vectorization (TF-IDF or BERT embeddings). "Garbage in, garbage out" — poor preprocessing = poor performance.

🌐 Neural Machine Translation (NMT)

Neural Machine Translation (NMT) uses encoder-decoder neural networks (now transformers) to translate text from one language to another end-to-end.
Encoder-Decoder Architecture:
• Encoder: Reads source sentence, creates context representations
• Decoder: Generates target sentence word-by-word using encoder context
• Used in Seq2Seq models (earlier RNN/LSTM based)
Attention in NMT:
The decoder attends to different encoder outputs at each generation step.
Allows the model to focus on relevant source words when generating each target word.
"Attention is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) replaced Seq2Seq with full transformer.

Transformer Attention Tricks in NMT

  • Self-Attention: Each layer combines words with others in the same sequence
  • Multi-headed Attention: 8 attention heads function independently
  • Normalized Dot-product Attention: Remove bias in dot product when using large networks (divide by √dₖ)
  • Positional Encodings: Ensures position distinguishability without RNN
  • Masking for Training: Mask future positions during decoder training for efficient batch training
BLEU Score (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy):
Standard evaluation metric for machine translation.
Measures overlap between generated translation and reference translation.
BLEU = Brevity Penalty × exp(Σ wₙ log pₙ)
where pₙ = n-gram precision
Range: 0 to 1 (higher = better, but not perfect metric)
💡 Getting Started with NMT Find training data (e.g., TED talks from IWSLT) → Download toolkit (OpenNMT, fairseq, Sockeye) → Train → Calculate BLEU score → Analyze what's right/wrong.
M7
Text Summarization
Extractive, Abstractive Methods & BERTSUM

📝 What is Text Summarization?

Text Summarization is the process of automatically distilling a large document into a shorter version that retains the most relevant information. By extracting key concepts and maintaining the original meaning, these systems can revolutionize industries such as banking, law, and healthcare.

Why Text Summarization Matters

  • Information Overload Management: Process growing volumes of text
  • Time Efficiency: Read summaries instead of full documents
  • Accessibility: Makes complex or lengthy content more approachable
  • Enhanced Search and Retrieval: Better indexing with concise summaries
  • Cost Reduction: Distills text to most essential points
Extractive Summarization

Copies the most important sentences directly from the source text. A subset of the original document — every word must appear in the source.

Methods: Statistical (TF-IDF), Graph-Based (TextRank), ML (sentence scoring), BERT-based (BERTSUM)
Abstractive Summarization

Generates NEW sentences and phrases to express the core ideas. A paraphrase — the summary may contain vocabulary NOT found in the source.

Methods: Seq2Seq architectures, BART, T5 (Transformer-based Deep Learning)

📌 Extractive Summarization Pipeline

Extractive summarization selects the most important sentences from the original document without modifying them.

Phase 1: Pre-processing and Representation

  1. Text Cleaning & Normalization: Standard data cleaning — lowercase, remove noise, handle special characters
  2. Sentence Segmentation: Split document into individual sentences using spaCy or NLTK
  3. Feature Engineering / Vectorization: Each sentence transformed into a numerical vector (TF-IDF embedding), linguistic/statistical features extracted

Phase 2: Scoring and Ranking

  1. Sentence Scoring (Feature-Based): Calculate raw score by aggregating feature weights — Position (first/last sentences = important), Length, Keyword Density
  2. Inter-Sentence Relationship Scoring: Calculate connectivity using graph theory or deep contextual similarity
  3. Final Score Aggregation & Ranking: Combine all scores (statistical + positional + relational), sort sentences

Phase 3: Selection and Assembly

  1. Top-K Selection (Redundancy Check): Select top K sentences based on final ranking; eliminate near-duplicates (redundancy check)
  2. Final Summary Assembly: Re-order selected sentences in original document order; join to form final summary

Techniques for Extractive Summarization

Technique Method Algorithm
Statistical Mathematical models evaluate word/sentence importance TF-IDF, LSA (Latent Semantic Analysis)
Graph-Based Sentences as nodes, edges weighted by similarity TextRank, LexRank
Machine Learning Supervised models trained on labeled (sentence, include?) data SVM, CRF, Neural classifiers
Transformer-Based Deep language understanding for sentence selection BERTSUM
TF-IDF Scoring for Extractive Summarization:
Each sentence's importance score = sum of TF-IDF values of its words.
Higher score = more informative sentence.
Rank sentences by score → select top-3 → reorder in original sequence → extractive summary.

✍️ Abstractive Summarization

Abstractive Summarization generates new text that captures the main ideas of the source, often using words and phrases not in the original. More human-like but harder to implement.
Seq2Seq Architecture:
Encoder reads source text, decoder generates summary word by word.
Uses attention mechanism to focus on relevant source parts.
Models: BART, T5, Pegasus
BART (Bidirectional & Auto-Regressive Transformer):
Encoder: bidirectional (like BERT)
Decoder: autoregressive (like GPT)
Pre-trained with denoising objectives — excellent for summarization.
Property Extractive Abstractive
Words in summary Must be from source Can be new words
Approach Selection Generation
Complexity Simpler (ranking problem) Complex (generation problem)
Methods TF-IDF, TextRank, BERTSUM BART, T5, Pegasus
Faithfulness High (direct quotes) Can hallucinate
Readability May be choppy/disconnected More coherent and natural

🧠 BERTSUM — Smart Sentence Highlighter

BERTSUM is a BERT-based extractive summarization model that dramatically improves accuracy of sentence selection by understanding deep semantic context — as opposed to simple statistical measures (TF-IDF, LexRank).

Why BERTSUM over Traditional Methods?

Traditional Method Weakness: TF-IDF and LexRank only count word overlap or look at sentence position. They often miss the true meaning and select sentences that are important but redundant (same info twice) or grammatically awkward.
BERTSUM Advantage: Reads the document and creates a deep semantic vector for each sentence, understanding its meaning in relation to the ENTIRE document. Treats sentence selection as binary classification.

BERTSUM Architecture

Architecture: Encoder-Only (uses pre-trained BERT)
Goal: Binary Classification — should sentence i be in the summary? (Yes/No)

Step 1 — Sentence Tagging:
Insert special [CLS] token at the beginning of EVERY sentence. The [CLS] token acts as the "representative" for its entire sentence.

Step 2 — Contextual Encoding:
BERT encoder processes the entire document at once. Output vector for each [CLS] token = contextual representation of that sentence.

Three Embedding Layers (Input Adaptation)

Embedding Type Purpose
Token Embeddings Vector representation of actual words/tokens
Interval Segment Embeddings Key difference from original BERT — alternating embeddings to distinguish sentences and learn inter-sentence relationships
Position Embeddings Absolute position of each token in the entire input sequence
Output:
• Tᵢ vectors (from [CLS] tokens) = contextual representations of sentences
• Tᵢ → Summarization Layer (classifier) → Yᵢ (probability ∈ [0,1])
• Yᵢ = probability that sentence i should be included in the extractive summary
• This is the binary classification step — sentences with Yᵢ > threshold are selected

Applications of BERTSUM

Legal & Regulatory Compliance
Summarize long contracts, court transcripts, SEC 10-K reports. Lawyers get core clauses immediately. Summary sentences are direct quotes — legally defensible and traceable to source.
Scientific Literature Review
Create quick summaries of research papers, clinical trials, technical patents. Researchers rapidly distill hypothesis, methodology, and conclusion without reading dozens of pages.

📊 ROUGE — Evaluation for Summarization

ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) is the standard evaluation metric for text summarization. It compares model-generated summaries with reference (human) summaries.
Metric What it Measures Formula
ROUGE-1 Unigram (word) overlap between generated and reference summary — content accuracy |overlap of unigrams| / |reference unigrams|
ROUGE-2 Bigram overlap — captures phrase-level accuracy |overlap of bigrams| / |reference bigrams|
ROUGE-L Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) — reflects sentence structure and meaning preservation LCS / |reference|
How ROUGE is used:
Run multiple summarization models (TF-IDF, TextRank, BERTSUM) on the same document.
Calculate ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L for each model against a reference (human-written) summary.
Rank models by score — higher ROUGE = summary closer to the reference.
⚠️ ROUGE Limitation ROUGE only measures surface overlap — doesn't capture semantic equivalence. A paraphrase with the same meaning but different words gets a low ROUGE score. For abstractive summarization, ROUGE is less reliable than for extractive.

1-Hour Quick Revision
The Ultimate Last-Minute Cheat Sheet

⚡ High-Yield Concepts & Formulas

1. NLP Processing Pipeline
• Tokenization: Splitting text into words/sentences. Fast, but ambiguous for un-segmented languages (Chinese).
• Normalization: Stemming (fast, heuristic-based, removes suffixes) vs Lemmatization (slower, dictionary-based, finds root lemma).
• Morphology: Inflectional (does not change POS, e.g. run→runs) vs Derivational (changes POS/meaning, e.g. happy→happiness).
2. Finite State Automata (FSA) & FSTs
• FSA: Recognizes/generates strings. Defined by states, start state, final states, transitions, alphabet.
• FST: Maps between 2 tapes (Lexical/Deep ↔ Surface). Used for morphological parsing.
• Composition: FSTs can be chained (e.g. Lexicon FST → Morphographemic FST).
3. Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging
• HMM (Generative): Models joint probability P(W, T).
  P(w|t) = Emission Prob. (Tag → Word)
  P(t|t-1) = Transition Prob. (Tag → Tag)
• CRF (Discriminative): Models conditional probability P(T|W) using global sequence context. Fixes HMM independence assumption flaws.
4. Language Models (N-Grams)
• Markov Assumption: Next word depends only on previous (N-1) words.
  P(w_n | w_1...w_{n-1}) ≈ P(w_n | w_{n-N+1}...w_{n-1})
• Perplexity (PP): Inverse probability of test set normalized by words. Lower PP = Better model.
• Laplace Smoothing: Add-1 to prevent zero probabilities.
  P(w) = (Count(w) + 1) / (Total Words + V) (V = Vocab Size).
5. Term Frequency - Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) • TF: How often a word occurs in a document.
  TF(t,d) = Count of t in d / Total words in d
• IDF: How rare a word is across ALL documents.
  IDF(t,D) = log( Total Docs / Docs containing t )
• TF-IDF = TF × IDF (High for locally frequent but globally rare words).
6. Syntax & Parsing
• Context-Free Grammar (CFG): Defines sentence structure. Rules like S → NP VP. Can cause structural ambiguity.
• Constituency Parsing: Groups words into nested phrases (trees).
• Dependency Parsing: Links words via grammatical relations (arrows from head to dependent).
7. Transformers & Advanced Models
• Self-Attention: Weighs importance of all words in a sentence relative to each word. Allows parallelization.
• BERT: Encoder-only, Bidirectional. Pre-trained on MLM (predict missing word) & NSP (Next Sentence Prediction).
• BART/T5: Encoder-Decoder models. Ideal for text generation / abstractive summarization.
8. Text Summarization & Evaluation
• Extractive: Copies important sentences (TF-IDF scoring, Graph/TextRank, BERTSUM).
• Abstractive: Generates novel paraphrased text (Seq2Seq, BART).
• ROUGE Metric: Evaluates summaries by overlap with human reference.
  ROUGE-N = Overlap N-grams / Reference N-grams
  ROUGE-L = Longest Common Subsequence / Reference Length
